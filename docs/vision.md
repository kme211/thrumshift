# Thrumshift Vision

## Elevator Pitch

Thrumshift is a browser-based science fiction puzzle game that combines real-time biometric data with immersive station operations. Players maintain a remote orbital facility by diagnosing and repairing failing systems while adapting to changing physiological conditions.

The goal is not to simulate space combat—it is to make the player feel like the engineer responsible for keeping a lonely station alive.

---

# Player Fantasy

"I am the operator this station depends on."

Players shouldn't feel like they're solving abstract puzzles.

They should feel like they're operating real machinery inside a believable place.

---

# Core Pillars

## 1. The station is the main character.

Every subsystem has history.

Every console has a purpose.

Every room feels maintained by generations of operators.

The station should feel lived in rather than abandoned.

---

## 2. Repairs happen in places.

Subsystem failures belong to physical locations.

Players don't simply launch puzzles from menus—they travel to the affected subsystem, operate its console, perform repairs, and return to normal operations.

Navigation should remain lightweight and web-native rather than becoming a 3D walking simulator.

---

## 3. Machine state is always visible.

The interface exists to communicate the station's condition.

Displays continue showing useful telemetry during failures.

Warnings highlight problems instead of replacing information.

---

## 4. The player is trusted.

The interface rarely explains itself.

Players learn through repeated operation.

Complex systems become familiar over time.

---

## 5. Every interaction feels physical.

Buttons illuminate.

Switches remain in their new position.

Indicators change state.

Doors open.

Systems become different after interaction rather than showing temporary success messages.

---

## 6. Calm is the default.

Most of the station should feel quiet.

Failures are meaningful because they interrupt normal operation.

Escalation should happen gradually.

---

# Design Maxims

- The station communicates machine state, not user actions.
- Every display has a purpose.
- Motion explains system state.
- Controls communicate machine state.
- Existing instrumentation evolves before new UI appears.
- Repairs happen in places, not menus.
- Operators are trusted, not taught.
- Hardware is substantial; graphics are simple.
- The station feels maintained, not pristine.
- Alerts compete for attention in proportion to the action they require.
- Systems are named for what they do, not what they are.
- Spaces are labeled for operators, not for players.
- Each system owns its own sound. Emergencies layer together rather than restarting the soundscape.
- Labels describe what an operator is about to do.
- Lighting defines space before decoration does.
- Use task lighting, ambient lighting, and accent lighting to communicate purpose, navigation, and mood.

---

# Visual Direction

The station draws inspiration from late-1960s through early-1980s industrial science fiction.

Primary influences include:

- Alien
- The Andromeda Strain

The goal is not nostalgia.

The goal is believable industrial technology.

Characteristics:

- CRT-inspired displays
- restrained scanlines
- monochrome and limited color palettes
- illuminated hardware controls
- service panels
- maintenance labels
- asset identifiers
- handwritten technician notes
- industrial typography
- subdued lighting
- practical machinery

Modern accessibility and responsiveness take precedence over historical accuracy.

---

# Interaction Philosophy

Interfaces communicate system state.

Failures escalate instead of exploding.

Warnings accumulate.

Controls acknowledge state changes through the hardware itself.

Players should rarely encounter modal dialogs.

Whenever possible, information should be incorporated into existing instrumentation.

---

# Audio Philosophy

Audio reinforces machine state.

Mechanical sounds are preferred over synthetic UI effects.

Examples include:

- relays
- switches
- motors
- fans
- tape drives
- machinery hum
- restrained alarm tones

Silence is valuable.

A new sound should mean something changed.

---

# Narrative Direction

The station exists to serve the interests of its founder rather than humanity as a whole.

Players slowly uncover its history through everyday operation instead of exposition.

Story emerges from maintenance records, inspection stickers, subsystem naming, environmental details, and the changing condition of the station.

---

# Naming Principle

Thrumshift is the name of the game and broader setting. Individual facilities have their own identities, names, histories, and personalities.

---

# MVP

The MVP proves:

- Heart-rate driven gameplay is compelling.
- One repair loop is satisfying.
- Atmosphere can be achieved in the browser.
- The station feels believable.

The MVP intentionally avoids:

- exploration
- multiple subsystems
- collaboration
- narrative progression
- extensive world simulation

---

# Long-Term Vision

Eventually the station becomes a place players know by memory.

Failures occur in recognizable locations.

Subsystems develop personality.

Crew members leave traces of their work.

Future updates may introduce:

- multiple station areas
- subsystem travel
- persistent station state
- cooperative operator roles
- richer environmental storytelling

The experience should evolve from solving isolated puzzles into operating a living station.
